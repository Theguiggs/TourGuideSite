@@SCENE=seed-100-saint-malo-route-du-rhum-scene-0

Stand facing the basins, your back to the ramparts. Today, the water is calm, a few masts sway gently. The hulls rub softly against their moorings, the façades reflect in trembling patches, and the ramparts behind you look like a granite balcony set above the harbor. But one autumn in four, this quiet stretch of water becomes the center of the sailing world.

Every four years, in early November, dozens of racing yachts moor here, side by side: giant trimarans, round-the-world monohulls, small boats belonging to enthusiasts. Around them grows a city within the city — pontoons, screens, the smell of crêpes and resin — and a human tide flows down from the ramparts to come lay eyes on them. Visitors move slowly, noses tilted up toward the masts, as if inside a makeshift cathedral of carbon, halyards, and folded sails. This is the Route du Rhum. In a few days, all these sailors will set off alone, each on their own boat, without a crew, to cross the Atlantic all the way to Pointe-à-Pitre, in Guadeloupe. Some 3,500 nautical miles. Alone, yes, you heard right.

Where does this mad idea come from? From 1978. At the time, the great solo transatlantic race is English, and its organizers have just tightened its rules. On the French side, an advertising man, Michel Etevenon, senses the deal of the century: create a French transat, free, open to any boat, backed by Guadeloupean rum producers eager to promote their product. The course retraces the old trade route between the Antilles and the ports of the Channel. The name falls into place on its own: the Route du Rhum. It speaks at once of the cargo, the voyage, the Atlantic, and that touch of celebration that clings to Saint-Malo even under a low sky.

No one, that day, imagines the first edition will give birth to one of the most beautiful stories in sport — and one of the most tragic. That's the mix that makes this place so particular: here, the quay applauds, but it also knows the sea signs no promises. Look again at the mooring lines and the rusted rings: everything seems designed to hold fast, while the race speaks only of letting go. I'll keep both for you on this walk.

Climb up onto the rampart, just above the Grande Porte. Let's look at all this from higher up.

@@STITLE=Esplanade Saint-Vincent
From here, in race years, you'd be looking down over a forest of masts. Take a minute to get acquainted with this fleet, because it tells the whole range of human madness.

There are the Ultims, first: immense trimarans that, lifted by their foils, rise up and literally fly above the water — the fastest of them swallow the Atlantic in barely a week. Then the great offshore racing monohulls, the round-the-world ones. The Class40s, more modest, where professionals rub shoulders with very serious amateurs. And my favorite: the Rhum class, the boats of old-timers and dreamers, the spirit of 1978 in person.

What makes this race unique is that everyone crosses paths. During village week, the skippers push through the crowd to reach their boat; you can see the hulls up close, chat with a sailor in oilskins, get a cap signed. Then, the day before the start, the boats pass one by one through the lock that closes off the basins, applauded like bullfighters entering the ring, and go anchor out in the roadstead.

Follow the rampart to the west, above the rooftops. I'm taking you to see the sea of the start.

Lean over the parapet. Below, the plage de Bon-Secours and its seawater pool. Ahead of you, the roadstead, the islets, then the open sea.

Now, picture this landscape on a Sunday in November, a Rhum year. On these ramparts, a dense crowd, bundled up, flags, children on shoulders. Out at sea, the fleet tacks back and forth waiting for the hour. And to the west, that dark silhouette set on the horizon is the cap Fréhel: the competitors will skim close under its cliffs before turning south — down there too, the moor is black with people.

The cannon fires. The line releases, the bows accelerate, helicopters circle like flies. It's a strange moment, when you think about it: an entire city gathered to watch people leave.

An hour later, the horizon has swallowed everything. The city empties all at once, and for three weeks, everyone here will follow, morning and evening, the little beacons of the boats on the map, the way you check in on family news.

Come down off the rampart through the porte de Dinan, and walk to the long jetty that closes off the harbor. That's where the solitude begins.

Walk out on the jetty, as far as the wind will let you. This breakwater is the last handshake from land. On departure days, loved ones come here, wave their arms one last time, and watch a sail become a dot.

Let's talk about what awaits whoever is on board. Three weeks alone, sometimes much more. You never really sleep: naps of a few minutes, jolted awake by alarms, because a cargo ship or a squall can appear at any moment. You watch the instruments, you make repairs at night half-soaked, you talk to yourself, you sometimes cry. And in the gales, you're afraid — everyone says so, the greatest champions first of all.

So why do it? Everyone has their own answer. The taste for the open sea, glory, silence, a score to settle with life. This city, in any case, understands them without needing an explanation: it has always watched its sailors leave for far away and for a long time — its old Cape Horners used to be gone for years at a stretch.

Come back toward the ramparts, follow them north, and climb up to the big round tower at the northwest corner. I have a graver story to tell you up there.

November 1978, the first Route du Rhum. Among the competitors, a huge star: Alain Colas. Winner of the English transat in 1972, the first sailor to have completed a solo round-the-world voyage on a multihull. Three years earlier, a line had nearly torn off his foot during a maneuver; he was said to be finished. He sets off anyway, on his trimaran Manureva — Éric Tabarly's former boat, renamed with a Tahitian word that speaks of birds and journeys.

On November 16, in the middle of a storm off the Azores, his radio sends a last message. Then nothing more. Neither the boat nor the man will ever be found.

France holds its breath for weeks. The following year, a song will bear the name of the vanished boat — you may have already hummed it without knowing it was about him.

Since then, every edition sets off carrying this memory on board. The sea here makes legends, and it keeps a few of them for itself.

Now follow the north rampart, to the gate that opens onto the great bay. It's time to talk about those who come back.

Below you, the plage de l'Éventail and the whole bay where the fleet parades before departures. It's a very old habit: those who want the west set off from here. As early as 1534, Jacques Cartier left this roadstead with two ships to look for a passage to Asia — he would find Canada and its great river instead.

But the heiress I want to introduce you to is named Florence Arthaud. November 1990: on her trimaran Pierre 1er, she stands up to the storms and all the favorites, and wins the Route du Rhum. A woman, alone, ahead of every man — a first in a major solo transat. The press nicknames her "the little fiancée of the Atlantic." The nickname is too tame for her: she's a magnificent wild spirit, who survived a terrible car accident at seventeen and chose the sea against everyone's advice.

Twelve years later, another woman, Ellen MacArthur, would make her mark among the monohulls. The door Florence opened never closed again.

She died in 2015, far from the ocean, in a helicopter accident. Here, her name still makes voices drop a little.

Go through the gate and down onto the dike. The city's largest beach, for its most beautiful story.

@@STITLE=Plage du Sillon
Walk a little way along this dike. Three kilometers of sand: the plage du Sillon. On days of the highest tides, the sea leaps right over the parapet — but that's a story for another walk.

Here's the story I promised. November 1978, the first Route du Rhum. After three weeks at sea, everything comes down to two men who couldn't be more different. Michel Malinovsky, an experienced sailor, on a big, sleek racing monohull built for speed: Kriter V. And Mike Birch, a quiet Canadian, a former cowboy turned skipper, on a small yellow eleven-meter trimaran: Olympus. The little yellow boat shouldn't stand a chance against the big one.

In the early morning, within sight of Guadeloupe, Malinovsky sees himself as the winner. But the wind drops, and the trimaran, light as an insect, slides through where the monohull gets bogged down. Birch comes back, mile after mile, and almost overtakes him right on the line. Twenty-three days of racing. Ninety-eight seconds apart. One of the closest finishes in the history of sailing, and the founding legend of this race: from that day on, everyone knows that here, nothing is ever decided in advance.

Forty years later, in 2018, history stutters magnificently: Francis Joyon overtakes François Gabart in the final miles and wins by a mere seven minutes — after just seven days at sea, on those flying trimarans I told you about.

That's why, every four years, this city watches its madmen set off: because they bring it back stories no one else knows how to make.

My walk ends here, facing the bay. If you come back some November during the race, climb up on the ramparts and shout along with everyone else. Thank you for walking with me. Hold your course.

November 1978, eerste Route du Rhum. Onder de deelnemers, een grote ster: Alain Colas. Winnaar van de Engelse transat in 1972, eerste zeiler die een solo-wereldreis op een multihull voltooide. Drie jaar eerder had een lijn hem tijdens een manoeuvre bijna een voet afgerukt; men zei dat hij klaar was. Hij vertrekt toch, op zijn trimaran Manureva — het voormalige schip van Éric Tabarly, herdoopt met een Tahitiaanse naam die spreekt van vogels en reizen.

Op 16 november, midden in een storm bij de Azoren, stuurt zijn radio een laatste bericht. Dan niets meer. Noch de boot, noch de man zullen ooit worden teruggevonden.

Frankrijk houdt wekenlang zijn adem in. Het jaar daarop draagt een lied de naam van de verdwenen boot — misschien heb je hem al neuriën zonder te weten dat het over hem ging.

Sindsdien vertrekt elke editie met deze herinnering aan boord. De zee hier maakt legendes, en houdt er een paar voor zichzelf.

Volg nu de noordelijke stadsmuur, tot aan de poort die uitkomt op de grote baai. Het is tijd om te praten over degenen die terugkeren.

Onder je, het plage de l'Éventail en de hele baai waar de vloot paradeert voor de starts. Het is een heel oude gewoonte: wie het westen wil, vertrekt hiervandaan. Al in 1534 verliet Jacques Cartier deze rede met twee schepen om een doorgang naar Azië te zoeken — hij zou in plaats daarvan Canada en zijn grote rivier vinden.

Maar de erfgename die ik je wil voorstellen heet Florence Arthaud. November 1990: op haar trimaran Pierre 1er trotseert ze de stormen en alle favorieten, en wint de Route du Rhum. Een vrouw, alleen, vóór alle mannen — een primeur in een grote solo-transat. De pers noemt haar "het bruidje van de Atlantische Oceaan". De bijnaam is te braaf voor haar: ze is een prachtige wildebras, die op haar zeventiende een verschrikkelijk verkeersongeval overleefde en tegen alle adviezen in voor de zee koos.

Twaalf jaar later zal een andere vrouw, Ellen MacArthur, zich onderscheiden bij de monohulls. De deur die Florence opende, is nooit meer dichtgegaan.

Ze overleed in 2015, ver van de oceaan, bij een helikopterongeluk. Hier daalt bij haar naam nog altijd de stem een beetje.

Ga door de poort en daal af naar de dijk. Het grootste strand van de stad, voor het mooiste verhaal.

Loop een stukje over deze dijk. Drie kilometer zand: het plage du Sillon. Op dagen met springtij springt de zee over de borstwering — maar dat vertel ik je in een andere wandeling.

Hier is het beloofde verhaal. November 1978, eerste Route du Rhum. Na drie weken op de Atlantische Oceaan komt alles neer op twee mannen die niets gemeen hebben. Michel Malinovsky, een ervaren zeeman, op een grote, gestroomlijnde racemonohull, gebouwd voor snelheid: Kriter V. En Mike Birch, een stille Canadees, oud-cowboy geworden schipper, op een kleine gele trimaran van elf meter: Olympus. De kleine gele boot zou niets moeten kunnen beginnen tegen de grote.

Vroeg in de ochtend, in het zicht van Guadeloupe, ziet Malinovsky zichzelf al als winnaar. Maar de wind valt weg, en de trimaran, licht als een insect, glijdt daar waar de monohull vastloopt. Birch haalt in, mijl na mijl, en passeert hem bijna op de finish. Drieëntwintig dagen race. Achtennegentig seconden verschil. Een van de nipste aankomsten uit de geschiedenis van het zeilen, en de stichtende legende van deze race: sindsdien weet iedereen dat hier nooit iets bij voorbaat vaststaat.

Veertig jaar later, in 2018, herhaalt de geschiedenis zich schitterend: Francis Joyon passeert François Gabart in de laatste mijlen en wint met slechts zeven minuten verschil — na amper zeven dagen op zee, op die vliegende trimarans waar ik je over vertelde.

Daarom kijkt deze stad om de vier jaar toe hoe haar dwazen vertrekken: omdat ze haar verhalen terugbrengen die niemand anders weet te maken.

Mijn wandeling eindigt hier, met uitzicht op de baai. Als je ooit terugkomt in een novembermaand van de race, klim dan op de stadsmuren en schreeuw mee met de anderen. Dank je voor het wandelen met mij. Hou koers.
