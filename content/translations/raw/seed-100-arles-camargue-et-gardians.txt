Climb up to this church, right at the top of the old town, on the esplanade overlooking the roofs. Notre-Dame-de-la-Major. Turn your back on the portal and look south: in the distance the plain opens out, flat, immense, as far as a line of water and sky. That is where the Camargue begins, that great wild delta where the Rhône flows into the sea.

And it is no accident that I bring you to this church. It belongs to the gardians. The gardians are the herdsmen of the Camargue bulls, the horsemen of the delta. They have had their brotherhood here for over five centuries — the confrérie de Saint-Georges, founded in 1512, one of the oldest in France. Every first of May they ride up here on horseback, by the hundred, in costume, for their great festival, blessed in front of this door.

Picture them. The man sitting straight in his saddle, the long trident in his hand, on his small white horse born in the marshes. He spends his life outdoors, in the wind and the salt, watching over herds of black bulls called manades. He lives in a low, whitewashed cabin, roofed with reeds.

Here is my thread through this walk. Arles is not only a town of old stones. It is the capital of a country apart, a kind of nation unto itself: the Camargue. Its bulls, its horsemen, its women in costume, its pride. I am going to help you discover it, street by street. Follow me, we go down towards the arena.

Here we are in front of the great arena. Forget the Romans who built it for a moment — that is another walk. What matters here is what happens in it today, when the tiers fill up for the bulls.

For the real local festival is not the Spanish corrida, even though Arles still holds some. It is the course camarguaise. Listen closely, because it is quite an art. In the arena, a black bull, sharp, horns held high. And men dressed in white, the razeteurs, who run at him, brush past his horns, and try to unhook, with a small comb-like hook, a rosette of ribbons. The animal is not killed. It is challenged. It goes back out alive, and it is often the bull that becomes the star.

The greatest bulls become legends. Their names are known, their fights are told and retold, sometimes a statue is raised to them. Every summer, the arena crowns the champion of champions in a famous race, the Cocarde d'or. Here, the bull is not a decoration. It is a hero. Let's go out into the streets. Follow me.
@@SCENE=seed-100-arles-camargue-et-gardians-scene-2

Go down to this working-class square, lined with narrow façades: the place Voltaire. On an ordinary day it is quiet. But a few times a year, for the ferias, it goes wild — and the whole town with it.

Twice a year, at Easter and in autumn, Arles gives itself over to the bulls for several days. Music, packed squares, brass bands playing in the streets, crowds everywhere. And above all, the abrivado. Listen closely: in the morning the gardians come down from the marshes with a group of young bulls, pressed between the horses, and drive them at a gallop all the way to the arena, through the streets of the town. On the sides, young people try to make them break away, to break up the run. It is a din of hooves, of shouting, of dust.

The whole town then throbs to the rhythm of the animals. On those days you understand that here the bull is not a Sunday spectacle: it is a passion that grips everyone, from the old to the children. Let's head back towards the great boulevard. Follow me, the scenery is about to change.

Here we are on the boulevard des Lices, the great promenade of Arles. On Saturday morning it is covered by one of the finest markets in Provence, kilometres of stalls. And here, the whole delta invites itself into your basket.

Rice, first. Does that surprise you? The Camargue is one of the great rice-growing regions of France: on these flooded lands, between sea and river, a red rice, a wholegrain rice, is grown, found on every table around here. Then salt, the fleur de sel of the Camargue, gathered by hand from the great salt pans that turn pink in the sun.

And the bull. Yes, the same one as in the arena. Its silhouette appears on posters, shop signs and everyday objects. It sums up the power of the Camargue landscape, but also the closeness between Arles and the low lands of the delta. The boulevard then becomes a gentle border: on one side the town of stone, on the other the world of the manades, the horses and the wide horizons. Let's go down towards the heart of the town. Push open the door of this great old mansion, on the rue de la République: the Museon Arlaten. The name sounds Provençal, and that is deliberate. This is not a museum of kings or of paintings: it is the museum of life here, of the people of Arles and the Camargue. Costumes, furniture, santons, tools, festivals, beliefs, all the everyday life of former times.

It was created by a towering figure, a poet: Frédéric Mistral. At the end of the nineteenth century, Mistral watches this peasant, Provençal world disappearing, swallowed up by the railway, by schooling in French, by modern life. So he decides to save it — not in books, but in objects. He gathers everything he can, headdresses, cradles, costumes, and founds this museum.

It is said that he wanted it made by the people of Provence, for the people of Provence. A mirror held up to a people so it would not forget who it is. Let's go back outside. Just next door, this people has a face, and it is a woman's face. Follow me.

On a festival day, look for an Arlésienne in costume, and stop to really look at her. For this costume is one of the finest in France. A long skirt, a shawl crossed over the chest and carefully pinned, and on the hair pulled up, a velvet ribbon, the coiffe. All of it held together by precious jewellery passed down from mother to daughter, including a cross worn nowhere else.

This is no costume for tourists. It is a living pride. Every summer the town celebrates this costume with a great festival, where young girls put it on for the first time, solemnly. And every three years Arles elects a Reine d'Arles, a Queen of Arles: a young woman from here, chosen because she knows the language, the traditions, the history of her country, who will represent Provence for three years.

Here again, you feel the same determination: not to let what one is die out. Ancient Rome, Van Gogh's stay — other walks tell you about those. This one wears a costume and speaks Provençal. A few more steps to a square, in front of the statue of a man. Follow me, that is where it all comes together.

We end on this shaded square, in front of the statue of an old man in stone, hat and neatly trimmed beard: Frédéric Mistral. It was put up while he was still alive, and it is said he attended its unveiling himself, a little embarrassed to see himself already turned to stone.

This man, born very close by, spent his life defending one thing: the language and the soul of Provence. He wrote his poems not in French but in Provençal, the language of the people, of the gardians, of the farmers. He brought other writers together to save it. And in 1904 this earned him the Nobel Prize in Literature — a world prize for a language people called dying. He gave the Nobel money to his museum, the one you have just seen.

Look around you at everything we have passed. The gardians and their five-hundred-year-old brotherhood. The bulls, heroes of the arena. The streets shaking under the hooves of the feria. The rice, the salt, the gardiane stew. The costume and the Queen of Arles. A friend of Mistral's, a gardian-poet, the marquis de Baroncelli, would even go on to found, for this people of horsemen, what he called the gardian nation.

That is the secret of Arles. It is not only a town. It is the capital of a country with no borders on the maps, but very real in people's hearts: the Camargue. Its bulls, its white horses, its pink flamingos, its salt, its men in white.

Stay a moment on this square. Listen. Under the French you can still hear Provençal singing. Take care of yourself, my friend, and see you soon.
