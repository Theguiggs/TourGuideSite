Stop in front of this old stone building, at the entrance to the basin, where the harbor water meets the water of the open sea. This is the Lieutenance. A big chunk of fortress, squat, pierced by an arch: it's almost all that remains of the ramparts that once enclosed Honfleur.

Here lived the king's lieutenant, the man who commanded the town in His Majesty's name. And this arch, under which carts used to pass, was the old Caen gate, one of the entrances to the walled town.

Look at it closely, because this gate has a secret. It was, for thousands of men, the last image of France. The last wall of the country they saw before months, sometimes years, sometimes forever.

Because here's the story I'm going to tell you throughout this walk. This small, quiet harbor, with its boats and its terraces, was one of the great departure ports of the world. From here, sailors set off to found towns on the other side of the ocean. From here, every year, whole fleets set course for icy seas to fish for cod. And long before them, mapless discoverers had already set course for worlds no one in Europe had yet seen.

From this basin, a man left to found Quebec. His name was Champlain. We're going to follow his trail, and that of all the others.

Walk along the quay, to the right, along the Vieux Bassin. Follow me.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-1
@@STITLE=Le Vieux Bassin
Here you are along the Vieux Bassin, with its tall houses mirrored in the water. This postcard setting has a birth date. We're in 1681. The king is Louis XIV, and his great minister, Colbert, wants to make France a maritime power. He orders the port of Honfleur to be enlarged.

Part of the ramparts is torn down — that's why only the Lieutenance remains — this basin is dug out, lined with quays. And all around, merchants and shipowners have these narrow houses built, rising very high, sometimes six or seven stories, capped with slate. Narrow, because land on the quay was expensive. Tall, to house the family, the clerks, and the goods all under the same roof.

What you take for a pretty pleasure harbor was a trading machine. Wood, salt, fish, hides from America. A beehive. On these quays, there was shouting, loading, haggling in every language.

Continue along the basin, toward the far end, where the memory of a man who went very far is kept. Follow me.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-2
@@STITLE=Vieux Bassin, mémoire du départ de Champlain
It's here, somewhere on these quays, that the adventure of French Canada began. In 1608, a navigator boards a ship in Honfleur said to have been named the Don-de-Dieu. His name is Samuel de Champlain.

He crosses the Atlantic, sails up an immense river, the St. Lawrence, and on a promontory, founds an outpost he names Quebec. The town still exists today. It's now the heart of an entire French-speaking people in North America. And it all began here, from this basin.

Champlain didn't make the crossing once, but dozens of times. Honfleur was his base, his starting point, his port of return. There, they traded knives and cloth for beaver furs — because it was the European fashion for beaver hats that financed the conquest of Canada.

Think of what a departure like that meant. Weeks at sea on a wooden hull, the unknown at the end of it, and a fifty-fifty chance of never coming back. It took a fierce kind of faith to cast off the moorings.

Now head into the old town, behind the quays, to two great wooden buildings. Follow me.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-3
@@STITLE=Les Greniers à sel
Look at these vast buildings of stone and wood, almost cathedrals without a bell tower. These are the salt granaries. They were built under Louis XIV to store a single thing — but a vital one: salt.

Why so much salt? Because of cod. Every year, boats from Honfleur set off for Newfoundland, off the coast of Canada, onto the great fishing banks. These sailors were called the terre-neuvas — the Newfoundlanders. They'd be away for months, in the cold, the fog, and the swell, fishing for cod with a line.

But fish rots. So, on board, they'd gut it, clean it out, and bury it in salt to preserve it until they returned. No salt, no cod; no cod, no terre-neuvas. These granaries were therefore the real treasure of the port. Salt was kept under lock and key, because it was expensive, and the king taxed it heavily.

Cod fishing fed Honfleur for centuries. Generations of families lived by the rhythm of spring departures and autumn returns — when they came back.

Head back toward the quays, to an old stone church turned museum. Follow me.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-4
@@STITLE=Église Saint-Étienne
This stone church, the oldest in Honfleur, no longer holds mass. It's been turned into a maritime museum. Inside: ship models, navigation instruments, and the memory of all these harbor sailors.

Because behind the fine stories of departures lies the other truth. The sea took its share. Every winter, some boats didn't come back. A gale on the Newfoundland banks, a reef, a leak, and an entire crew would vanish. In the families of Honfleur, they counted the widows.

That's why faith held such a place. Before leaving, the sailors climbed up there, on the hill, to pray at the Notre-Dame-de-Grâce chapel. They'd leave a model of their ship there, as a promise: bring me back, and I'll offer you my ship in miniature. These ex-votos still hang from the chapel ceiling.

Leaving Honfleur wasn't a postcard adventure. It was a bet on your own life, renewed every season.

Follow the quay toward the harbor exit, where the water opens onto the open sea. Follow me.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-5
@@STITLE=Le port des découvreurs
Take a few more steps toward the outer harbor, where the boats head out to sea. And look out toward the open water, because Champlain and the terre-neuvas aren't the only ones who set off from here.

Long before them, Honfleur was already a port of explorers. In 1503, a local shipowner, Binot Paulmier de Gonneville, sets sail southward, very far, and reaches unknown lands — probably the coast of Brazil. He returns two years later with a young man from over there, a chief's son, whom he had promised to bring back home, and whom he was never able to return.

It's even said that, centuries earlier, sailors from this port pushed as far as the coasts of Africa. Proof is lacking, historians doubt it, so let's stay cautious. But this old story does say something true: here, people weren't afraid of the unknown.

Think of these men. No reliable map, no idea what awaited them at the end of the water. Just a hull, a sail, and the wild urge to see what lay beyond the horizon.

Walk to the end of the jetty, facing the sea and the great bridge. Our story ends there. Follow me.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-6

Here we are at the end of the port, facing the open sea. In front of you, the Seine estuary opens up wide, and there, immense, this white bridge suspended from its cables, spanning the river in a single leap: the Pont de Normandie. It opened in 1995. For a long time, it was among the largest bridges of its kind in the world. Today, it links Honfleur to Le Havre in a few minutes.

Take the measure of what you're looking at. This same channel, this same water flowing out to sea, is the route taken by everyone I've told you about. Champlain, heading for Quebec. The terre-neuvas, heading for the cod banks. De Gonneville, heading for Brazil. All of them passed through here, under the eyes of the Lieutenance, to melt into this horizon.

That's the secret of Honfleur. It isn't just the prettiest harbor in Normandy. It's a threshold. A gateway out of the known world. For centuries, ordinary men — sailors, fishermen, cadets without fortune — cast off their moorings here to go redraw the map of the Earth. Some founded cities. Others never came back. All of them looked, one last time, at this same stone arch.

The bridge has replaced the great crossings. But the water still flows out to the open sea. Stay a moment and watch it go. See you soon, friend.

Aquí vivía el teniente del rey, el hombre que mandaba la plaza en nombre de Su Majestad. Y ese arco, bajo el cual pasaban las carretas, era la antigua puerta de Caen, una de las entradas de la ciudad amurallada.

Mírala bien, porque esta puerta tiene un secreto. Fue, para miles de hombres, la última imagen de Francia. El último muro del país que veían antes de meses, a veces años, a veces para siempre.

Porque esta es la historia que voy a contarte a lo largo de este paseo. Este pequeño puerto tranquilo, con sus barcas y sus terrazas, fue uno de los grandes puertos de partida del mundo. De esta dársena, un hombre partió a fundar Quebec. Se llamaba Champlain. Vamos a seguir su huella, y la de todos los demás.

Continúa a lo largo de la dársena, hacia el fondo, allí donde se guarda la memoria de un hombre que partió muy lejos. Sígueme.

Es aquí, en algún lugar de estos muelles, donde comenzó la aventura del Canadá francés. En 1608, un navegante embarca en Honfleur en un buque que se dice llamado Don-de-Dieu. Se llama Samuel de Champlain.

Cruza el Atlántico, remonta un inmenso río, el San Lorenzo, y en un promontorio, funda un puesto que bautiza Quebec. La ciudad todavía existe. Es hoy el corazón de todo un pueblo francófono en América del Norte. Y todo eso partió de aquí, de esta dársena.

Champlain no hizo el viaje una sola vez, sino decenas. Honfleur era su base, su punto de partida, su puerto de regreso. Allá, se trocaban cuchillos y telas por pieles de castor —porque es la moda de los sombreros de castor, en Europa, la que financió la conquista de Canadá.

Piensa en lo que representaba una partida así. Semanas de mar sobre un casco de madera, lo desconocido al final, y una posibilidad entre dos de no volver jamás. Hacía falta una fe tremenda para soltar amarras.

Adéntrate ahora en el casco antiguo, detrás de los muelles, hasta dos grandes edificios de madera. Sígueme.

@@SCENE=seed-100-honfleur-port-des-explorateurs-scene-3
@@STITLE=Les Greniers à sel
Mira estos vastos edificios de piedra y madera, casi catedrales sin campanario. Son los graneros de sal. Se construyeron bajo Luis XIV para almacenar una sola cosa, pero una cosa vital: la sal.

¿Por qué tanta sal? Por culpa del bacalao. Cada año, barcos de Honfleur partían hacia Terranova, frente a las costas de Canadá, hacia los grandes bancos de peces. A esos marineros se les llamaba los terranovas. Se ausentaban meses, en el frío, la niebla y el oleaje, pescando bacalao con caña.

Pero un pescado se pudre. Así que, a bordo, lo abrían, lo vaciaban, y lo enterraban en sal para conservarlo hasta el regreso. Sin sal, no hay bacalao; sin bacalao, no hay terranovas. Estos graneros eran, pues, el verdadero tesoro del puerto. Se guardaba la sal bajo llave, porque valía cara y el rey la gravaba con fuerza.

La pesca del bacalao alimentó a Honfleur durante siglos. Generaciones de familias vivieron al ritmo de las partidas de primavera y los regresos de otoño —cuando regresaban.

Vuelve hacia los muelles, hasta una vieja iglesia de piedra convertida en museo. Sígueme.

@@STITLE=Église Saint-Étienne
Esta iglesia de piedra, la más antigua de Honfleur, ya no oficia misa. Se ha convertido en museo de la Marina. En el interior: maquetas de barcos, instrumentos de navegación, y el recuerdo de todos esos marineros del puerto.

Porque detrás de las bellas historias de partidas, está la otra verdad. El mar se cobraba su parte. Cada invierno, algunos barcos no regresaban. Un golpe de viento en los bancos de Terranova, un arrecife, una vía de agua, y toda una tripulación desaparecía. En las familias de Honfleur, se contaban las viudas.

Por eso la fe ocupaba tanto lugar. Antes de partir, los marineros subían allá arriba, a la colina, a rezar en la capilla Notre-Dame-de-Grâce. Dejaban ahí una maqueta de su navío, como una promesa: tráeme de vuelta, y te ofrezco mi barco en miniatura. Estos exvotos todavía cuelgan del techo de la capilla.

Partir de Honfleur no era una aventura de postal. Era una apuesta por la propia vida, renovada cada temporada.

En 1503, un armador de aquí, Binot Paulmier de Gonneville, levó velas hacia el sur, muy lejos, y alcanzó tierras desconocidas —sin duda la costa de Brasil. Regresó dos años después con un joven de allá, hijo de un jefe, a quien había prometido devolver a su casa, y a quien nunca pudo devolver.

Se cuenta incluso que, siglos antes, marineros de este puerto habrían llegado hasta las costas de África. Faltan pruebas, los historiadores lo dudan, así que seamos prudentes. Pero esta vieja historia sí dice algo verdadero: aquí, no se tenía miedo a lo desconocido.

Piensa en estos hombres. Sin mapa fiable, sin idea de lo que les esperaba al final del agua. Solo un casco, una vela, y las ganas locas de ver qué había detrás del horizonte.

Camina hasta el final del espigón, frente al mar y al gran puente. Nuestra historia termina ahí. Sígueme.

Todos miraron, una última vez, este mismo arco de piedra.

El puente ha reemplazado las grandes travesías. Pero el agua sigue fluyendo hacia el mar abierto. Quédate un momento mirándola partir. Y hasta pronto, amigo.
