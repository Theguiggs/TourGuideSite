@@TITLE=Paris — Montmartre of the Painters: the Hill That Invented Modern Art
@@DESC=Art — from Renoir to Picasso, the poor hill where the way of seeing was reinvented, from the Bateau-Lavoir to the cabarets.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-0
@@STITLE=Place Blanche
Stop for a second, under the red wings. That papier-mâché windmill turning above you is the Moulin Rouge, opened in 1889, the same year as the Eiffel Tower. At the foot of the Butte, where respectable Paris came to get a thrill. The cancan is invented here, people drink here, show off their legs here. And in a corner, a small man with broken legs is drawing. Toulouse-Lautrec. Out of these dancers, out of La Goulue, he makes posters that become, without anyone knowing it yet, art.

Here's the secret I want to tell you. Look at this hill in front of you. A century ago, it was almost nothing: a village of windmills perched above Paris, half in the countryside. Rents cost almost nothing. So the poor climbed up here. And among the poor, painters without a penny to their name: Renoir, Van Gogh, Picasso. Here, in the grime and the freedom, between two cabarets, they're going to do something enormous. They're going to invent modern art. Break a way of painting five centuries old, and change the way the whole world sees.

It all started from this small mound of earth. We're going to climb it together, lane by lane, in the footsteps of those who dared everything. Take the rue Lepic, on your right, and start climbing. I'll follow you.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-1
@@STITLE=54 rue Lepic
Catch your breath. You're at 54, rue Lepic. An ordinary façade, a balcony, nothing gives it away; but behind these windows lived Vincent van Gogh. From 1886 to 1888, two years, with his brother Theo, who sold paintings further down in Paris.

Vincent arrives from a dark Holland. His painting, up until then, is earth, peasants, muddy tones. And then he moves up here. He meets the Impressionists, the light of Paris, other people's colors. Something ignites. His palette catches fire. The yellows, the blues, the vibrating brushstrokes, everything that will make the Van Gogh the world adores, it begins in this apartment, on this hill. He paints the view from his window, he paints the windmills right above, he paints Paris at his feet.

Two years later, he'll head south, toward Arles and the sun that will burn him. But it's here, on rue Lepic, that he learned color. People walk past it without knowing. You, now, you know.

Keep climbing rue Lepic. It turns, it winds. At the very top, a real windmill awaits you, the last survivor of a time when the Butte had dozens of them.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-2
@@STITLE=Le Moulin de la Galette
There it is. This wooden windmill perched on its hill is the Moulin de la Galette. Well, two remain, the one you see and another a little further on. They used to grind Montmartre's grain and gypsum. Then the millers had an idea: open a guinguette, an open-air dance hall, where Paris workers came up to dance on Sundays.

One day in 1876, a painter sets up his easel here. Auguste Renoir. He watches the crowd dancing under the trees, the patches of sunlight falling through the leaves onto the dresses and hats. And he paints this: not a frozen scene, but light in motion, joy, movement, the blur of a happy Sunday. Le Bal du moulin de la Galette. One of the most beautiful Impressionist paintings in the world, today at the Musée d'Orsay.

That's the revolution beginning. No longer painting kings or gods, but ordinary people dancing, everyday life caught in full light. Van Gogh will paint this windmill, Toulouse-Lautrec too, Picasso again. One windmill, three geniuses.

Now head back down toward the place Émile-Goudeau. A wooden building awaits us, the most important in the entire history of modern art.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-3
@@STITLE=Le Bateau-Lavoir
You're on the place Émile-Goudeau, in front of a very plain façade. Don't be fooled by it. Here once stood the Bateau-Lavoir, a wooden shack full of wretched studios, with no water or heating. A poet, Max Jacob, nicknamed it that because it creaked and swayed like the laundry boats on the Seine.

In this hovel, around 1904, a poor twenty-three-year-old Spaniard moves in, painting by candlelight at night. Pablo Picasso. It's there, in a filthy room of this shack, that he paints in 1907 a painting that will blow everything apart: Les Demoiselles d'Avignon. Five women with broken faces, carved like masks, seen from several angles at once. His friends are shocked, some think he's gone mad. He has just killed three centuries of painting. With Braque, right after, he invents Cubism, the greatest rupture in Western art.

The Bateau-Lavoir burned down in 1970; what you see is a reconstruction. But the place remains sacred. Modigliani, Juan Gris lived here too. A shack of planks, and the twentieth century being born inside it.

Head back up toward the summit. A square full of painters awaits you: the place du Tertre.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-4
@@STITLE=Place du Tertre
Here we are: the place du Tertre, the heart of the old village. Today you'll find it packed with people, ringed with painters who'll do your portrait in ten minutes and terraces full of tourists. Don't shrug it off too fast. Look instead at what lies beneath it.

This square was the main square of the town of Montmartre, back when the Butte was its own commune, with its own town hall and mayor. And throughout the nineteenth century, the real painters lived and worked around here, for lack of anything better. Setting up an easel outdoors, painting from life, in the open air: this habit was born with the Impressionists, and it took root on this square. Today's portrait artists, however commercial they may be, are the great-grandchildren of that new idea: the studio is the street.

They say that here, and in the nearby cabarets, every broke genius Paris had to offer crossed paths, argued, helped each other out. Picasso, Utrillo, Modigliani, a few meters apart from one another. Paris's poorest village was the world capital of painting.

Take the small rue Poulbot, just alongside. We're going to visit another revolutionary of the eye: Salvador Dalí.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-5
@@STITLE=Espace Dalí
Go down these few steps, rue Poulbot. Behind these walls hides the Espace Dalí, the only place in France entirely devoted to Salvador Dalí. Sculptures, engravings, melting clocks, elephants on spider legs: the whole universe of the Catalan master of Surrealism.

You might say: Dalí is Spain, Catalonia, not Montmartre. That's true. But his presence here tells us something. After the Impressionists, after the Cubists, modern art never stopped reinventing itself, and each new wave came looking for a bit of the Butte's legend. Surrealism is the next generation: it's no longer just about breaking form, it's about opening the doors of dream and the unconscious. Melting clocks are time that no longer obeys.

By the way, the street bears the name of Francisque Poulbot, a cartoonist who drew Montmartre's poor kids, scruffy and mischievous: the "poulbots." You can still see them on signs and façades. The Butte invented sophisticated art, and also its popular, tender, slightly cheeky image.

Now we climb north, rue Cortot, toward the oldest house on the hill, the one where Renoir had his studio.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-6
@@STITLE=Musée de Montmartre
At 12, rue Cortot, push open the gate. This house is the oldest on the Butte, a seventeenth-century manor, with a garden overlooking the slope. Today it's the Musée de Montmartre, and it might be the address most inhabited by painters in all of Paris.

Count up who worked here. Renoir first, who rented a studio within these walls at the very moment he was painting the Bal du moulin de la Galette, just steps away. Then Suzanne Valadon, an incredible case: a former model, an acrobat who fell from a trapeze, she learns painting by watching herself pose for Renoir and Degas, and becomes one of the great artists of her time, a woman, in this world of men. She lives here with her son, Maurice Utrillo, the child of the Butte, alcoholic and brilliant, who will paint the streets of Montmartre better than anyone.

The garden has been redesigned to look like Renoir's canvases. Sit down for a minute if you can: it's exactly the light he was looking for.

Come back out and look across the way. On the other side of the street, on the slope, a few rows of vines still cling on. Follow me to the Clos Montmartre.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-7
@@STITLE=Le Clos Montmartre
There they are, these few rows of plants clinging to the slope, between two buildings: the Clos Montmartre. This little plot is a surprise, right in the middle of Paris. But above all, it's the living memory of what the Butte was for centuries: a hill of crops and windmills, still separated from the big city by paths and open land.

The current plot isn't medieval, rest assured: it was laid out in 1933, to save the land from developers and keep a piece of countryside in Montmartre. So it tells less of a production story than an act of urban planning: preserving an empty space, a slope, and a memory in the middle of the buildings.

Why am I stopping you in front of three rows of vines? Because everything is right there. If the painters climbed up here, it's because of this countryside: far from the city, outside the taxes, with rents worth nothing and cabarets where people sang. Poverty and vineyards made the genius. Without this poor hill, no modern art.

Go down the rue des Saules. At the bottom, a little house and a legendary cabaret.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-8
@@STITLE=Au Lapin Agile
Look at this low little house, with its tree and its sign: a rabbit leaping out of a saucepan. This is the cabaret Au Lapin Agile, one of the last authentic cabarets in Paris, still active today. Its name comes from a play on words. The sign, that rabbit jumping out of a pot, was painted by an illustrator named André Gill: the "lapin à Gill" (Gill's rabbit) became, to the ear, the "Lapin Agile" (nimble rabbit).

At the start of the twentieth century, it's the haunt of the Butte's penniless artists. People drink little here, sing, recite verses, remake the world until dawn. Picasso comes here, and paints a famous picture from it. Apollinaire, Utrillo, Mac Orlan push open its door.

It's from here, so the story still goes, that a memorable prank was launched. Writers tied a paintbrush to a donkey's tail, let it beat a canvas with its tail dipped in paint, and presented the result in a real salon under a pompous title. No one saw through it. A joke to mock modern art, played by the very people who were inventing it.

Walk back up a few steps. A pink house at the corner of the street, you can't miss it.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-9
@@STITLE=La Maison Rose
There it is, la Maison Rose. A small building with pink walls and green shutters, set at the corner of the rue de l'Abreuvoir and the rue des Saules. It's probably the most photographed house in Montmartre, and above all the most painted.

Because it was Maurice Utrillo who made it immortal. Suzanne Valadon's son, the child of the Butte, painted this street corner dozens of times, with its white skies and its slightly leaning walls. It's said the house owes its color to a friend of Picasso's circle, who supposedly had it painted pink at the start of the century. It hasn't changed since.

Take the time to look around you. These lanes running downhill, these staircases, these streetlamps, these crooked walls: this is the Montmartre the painters fixed for eternity. You're not just walking through a pretty neighborhood. You're walking inside hundreds of paintings hung in museums all over the world. The subject and the setting have become one and the same.

Now, turn toward the summit. That white dome towering over everything is our last stop. Climb with me toward the Sacré-Cœur.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-10
@@STITLE=Le Sacré-Cœur
Here we are, right at the top. The Sacré-Cœur, this great white vessel visible from all of Paris. It was built slowly, from 1875 to 1914, after the defeat of 1870 and the crushing of the Commune, like a vow, a penance in stone. The painters of the Butte, for their part, didn't like it: too new, too white, too proper, planted above their bohemia like a reproach. They turned their backs on it to paint the windmills and the vines instead.

And yet, come here, onto the forecourt. Look. All of Paris is at your feet, as far as the eye can see, in mist or in sunshine. This is the view they had every single day, these brilliant starving artists: Renoir, Van Gogh, Picasso, Utrillo, Valadon. They lived in filth, on a hill of vineyards and cabarets, and they saw the world from higher up than anyone else.

Think back over the path. The cancan and Lautrec down below; Van Gogh's color on rue Lepic; Renoir's dance; Picasso's Demoiselles in a shack of planks; the cabarets, the vines, la Maison Rose. On this small mound of earth, within a few decades, penniless men and women reinvented the very way of seeing the world. They left, one day, for Montparnasse and for glory. But the Butte remained, and the art it gave birth to hangs everywhere, on every wall on the planet.

Thank you for climbing all the way up here with me. Go back down slowly, eyes wide open. Every lane, from now on, is a painting. See you soon on the Butte.

Vincent llega de una Holanda sombría. Su pintura, hasta entonces, es la tierra, los campesinos, los tonos de barro. Y luego sube aquí. Conoce a los impresionistas, la luz de París, los colores de los demás. Algo se enciende. Su paleta prende fuego. Los amarillos, los azules, las pinceladas que vibran, todo lo que hará el Van Gogh que el mundo adora, empieza en este apartamento, en esta colina. Ahí está. Este molino de madera encaramado en su colina es el Moulin de la Galette. Bueno, quedan dos, el que ves y otro un poco más allá. Antaño molían el grano y el yeso de Montmartre. Luego a los molineros se les ocurrió una idea: abrir una guinguette, un baile al aire libre, donde los obreros de París subían a bailar los domingos.

Un día de 1876, un pintor planta su caballete aquí. Auguste Renoir. Mira a la multitud que baila bajo los árboles, las manchas de sol que caen a través de las hojas sobre los vestidos y los sombreros. Y pinta eso: no una escena inmóvil, sino la luz que se mueve, la alegría, el movimiento, el desenfoque de un domingo feliz. Le Bal du moulin de la Galette. Uno de los cuadros impresionistas más bellos del mundo, hoy en el musée d'Orsay.

Esa es la revolución que comienza. Dejar de pintar reyes y dioses, y pintar a gente sencilla que baila, la vida ordinaria atrapada en plena luz. Van Gogh pintará este molino, Toulouse-Lautrec también, Picasso también. Un molino, tres genios.

Baja ahora hacia la place Émile-Goudeau. Nos espera un edificio de madera, el más importante de toda la historia del arte moderno.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-3
@@STITLE=Le Bateau-Lavoir
Estás en la place Émile-Goudeau, delante de una fachada muy sencilla. No te fíes de ella. Aquí se alzaba el Bateau-Lavoir, una barraca de madera llena de talleres miserables, sin agua ni calefacción. Un poeta, Max Jacob, la apodó así porque crujía y se balanceaba como los bateaux-lavoirs del Sena.

En ese tugurio, hacia 1904, se instala un joven español de veintitrés años, sin un céntimo, que pinta de noche a la luz de una vela. Pablo Picasso. Es ahí, en una habitación sucia de esa chabola, donde pinta en 1907 un cuadro que va a hacerlo estallar todo: Les Demoiselles d'Avignon. Cinco mujeres de rostros rotos, tallados como máscaras, vistas desde varios lados a la vez. Sus amigos se escandalizan, algunos creen que se ha vuelto loco. Acaba de matar tres siglos de pintura. Con Braque, en la misma época, inventa el cubismo, la mayor ruptura del arte occidental.

El Bateau-Lavoir ardió en 1970; lo que ves es una reconstrucción. Pero el lugar sigue siendo sagrado. Modigliani, Juan Gris vivieron también aquí. Una chabola de tablones, y el siglo veinte naciendo dentro.

Sube de nuevo hacia la cima. Te espera una plaza llena de pintores: la place du Tertre.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-4
@@STITLE=Place du Tertre

Esta plaza era la plaza mayor del pueblo de Montmartre, cuando la Butte era un municipio aparte, con su ayuntamiento y su alcalde. Y durante todo el siglo diecinueve, los verdaderos pintores vivieron y trabajaron por aquí, a falta de algo mejor. Poner el caballete fuera, pintar del natural, al aire libre: esa costumbre nació con los impresionistas, y echó raíces en esta plaza. Los retratistas de hoy, por muy comerciales que sean, son los tataranietos de esa idea nueva: el taller es la calle.

Se cuenta que aquí y en los cabarets vecinos, todo lo que París tenía de genios sin blanca se cruzaba, discutía, se ayudaba mutuamente. Picasso, Utrillo, Modigliani, a pocos metros unos de otros. El pueblo más pobre de París era la capital mundial de la pintura.

Toma la pequeña rue Poulbot, justo al lado. Vamos a visitar a otro revolucionario de la mirada: Salvador Dalí.

Mi dirai: Dalí è la Spagna, la Catalogna, non Montmartre. È vero. Ma la sua presenza qui racconta qualcosa. Dopo gli impressionisti, dopo i cubisti, l'arte moderna non ha mai smesso di reinventarsi, e ogni nuova ondata è venuta a cercare un po' della leggenda della Butte. Il surrealismo è la generazione successiva: non si tratta più solo di rompere la forma, si aprono le porte del sogno e dell'inconscio. Gli orologi che si sciolgono sono il tempo che non obbedisce più.

Di passaggio, la via porta il nome di Francisque Poulbot, un disegnatore che ha ritratto i bambini poveri di Montmartre, spettinati, birichini: i «poulbots». Li si vede ancora su insegne e facciate. La Butte ha inventato l'arte colta, e anche la sua immagine popolare, tenera, un po' canagliesca.

Ora saliamo verso nord, rue Cortot, verso la casa più antica della collina, quella dove Renoir aveva il suo atelier.

@@STITLE=Musée de Montmartre

Fai il conto di chi ha lavorato qui. Renoir, per primo, che affittava un atelier tra queste mura proprio mentre dipingeva il Bal du moulin de la Galette, a due passi. Poi Suzanne Valadon, un caso incredibile: ex modella, acrobata caduta da un trapezio, impara a dipingere osservando se stessa posare per Renoir e Degas, e diventa una delle grandi artiste del suo tempo, una donna, in quel mondo di uomini. Vive qui con suo figlio, Maurice Utrillo, il bambino della Butte, alcolizzato e geniale, che dipingerà le strade di Montmartre meglio di chiunque altro.

Il giardino è stato ridisegnato come nelle tele di Renoir. Siediti un minuto se puoi: è esattamente la luce che lui cercava.

Esci di nuovo e guarda di fronte. Dall'altra parte della strada, sul pendio, alcune file di vigne si aggrappano ancora. Seguimi fino al Clos Montmartre.

@@STITLE=Le Clos Montmartre
Eccole, queste poche file di piante aggrappate al pendio, tra due edifici: il Clos Montmartre. Questo piccolo terreno sorprende in piena Parigi. L'attuale appezzamento non è medievale, sta' tranquillo: fu sistemato nel 1933, per salvare il terreno dai costruttori e conservare a Montmartre un pezzo di campagna. Racconta dunque meno una produzione che un atto di urbanistica: preservare un vuoto, un pendio e una memoria in mezzo agli edifici.

Perché ti fermo davanti a tre ceppi di vite? Perché tutto è lì. Se i pittori sono saliti qui, è a causa di questa campagna: lontano dalla città, fuori dalle tasse, con affitti da nulla e cabaret dove si cantava. La miseria e le vigne hanno fatto il genio. Senza questa collina povera, niente arte moderna.

Scendi rue des Saules. In fondo, una casetta e un cabaret leggendario.

Guarda questa casetta bassa, con il suo albero e la sua insegna: un coniglio che salta fuori da una pentola. È il cabaret Au Lapin Agile, uno degli ultimi cabaret autentici di Parigi, ancora in attività. Il suo nome viene da un gioco di parole. L'insegna, quel coniglio che salta da una pentola, è stata dipinta da un disegnatore chiamato André Gill: il «lapin à Gill» (il coniglio di Gill) è diventato, a orecchio, il «Lapin Agile» (il coniglio agile).

All'inizio del ventesimo secolo, è il rifugio degli artisti squattrinati della Butte. Si beve poco, si canta, si recitano versi, si rifà il mondo fino all'alba. Picasso ci viene, e ne trae un quadro famoso. Apollinaire, Utrillo, Mac Orlan ne spingono la porta.

È da qui, si racconta ancora, che partì uno scherzo memorabile. Alcuni scrittori legano un pennello alla coda di un asino, lo lasciano battere una tela con la sua coda intinta nella pittura, e presentano il risultato in un vero salone sotto un titolo altisonante. Nessuno ci vede nulla di strano. Uno scherzo per prendere in giro l'arte moderna, fatto proprio da coloro che la stavano inventando.

Risali di qualche passo. Una casa rosa all'angolo della strada, non puoi mancarla.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-9

Eccola, la Maison Rose. Un piccolo edificio dai muri rosa e dalle imposte verdi, piantato all'angolo tra rue de l'Abreuvoir e rue des Saules. È probabilmente la casa più fotografata di Montmartre, e soprattutto la più dipinta.

Perché fu Maurice Utrillo a renderla immortale. Il figlio di Suzanne Valadon, il bambino della Butte, dipinse quest'angolo di strada decine di volte, con i suoi cieli bianchi e i suoi muri un po' storti. Si racconta che la casa debba il suo colore a un'amica del circolo di Picasso, che l'avrebbe fatta dipingere di rosa all'inizio del secolo. Da allora non è più cambiata.

Prenditi il tempo di guardarti intorno. Questi vicoli che scendono, queste scale, questi lampioni, questi muri storti: è la Montmartre che i pittori hanno fissato per l'eternità. Non stai semplicemente camminando in un bel quartiere. Stai camminando dentro centinaia di quadri appesi nei musei di tutto il mondo. Il soggetto e lo scenario non fanno più che uno.

Ora, girati verso la cima. Quella cupola bianca che domina tutto è la nostra ultima tappa. Sali con me verso il Sacré-Cœur.

@@SCENE=seed-100-paris-montmartre-des-peintres-scene-10
@@STITLE=Le Sacré-Cœur
Eccoci, proprio in cima. Il Sacré-Cœur, questo grande vascello bianco che si vede da tutta Parigi. Fu costruito lentamente, dal 1875 al 1914, dopo la sconfitta del 1870 e la repressione della Comune, come un voto, una penitenza di pietra. I pittori della Butte, dal canto loro, non lo amavano: troppo nuovo, troppo bianco, troppo perbene, piantato sopra la loro bohème come un rimprovero. Gli voltavano le spalle per dipingere i mulini e le vigne.

Eppure, vieni qui, sul sagrato. Guarda. Tutta Parigi è ai tuoi piedi, a perdita d'occhio, nella foschia o nel sole. Questa è la vista che avevano ogni giorno, quei geni morti di fame: Renoir, Van Gogh, Picasso, Utrillo, Valadon. Vivevano nel sudiciume, su una collina di vigne e cabaret, e vedevano il mondo da più in alto di chiunque altro.

Ripensa al cammino percorso. Il cancan e Lautrec là in basso; il colore di Van Gogh in rue Lepic; il ballo di Renoir; le Demoiselles di Picasso in una baracca di assi; i cabaret, le vigne, la Maison Rose. Su questo piccolo cumulo di terra, in pochi decenni, uomini e donne senza soldi hanno reinventato lo stesso modo di vedere il mondo. Se ne sono andati, un giorno, verso Montparnasse e verso la gloria. Ma la Butte è rimasta, e l'arte a cui ha dato vita è appesa ovunque, su tutti i muri del pianeta.

Grazie per essere salito fin qui con me. Ridiscendi piano, con gli occhi ben aperti. Ogni vicolo, ormai, è un quadro. A presto sulla Butte.
